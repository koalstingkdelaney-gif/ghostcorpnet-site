    Why a Tabletop Exercise, and Why an Unrehearsed Plan Is Not a Plan

    You already have an incident response plan for your AI agents. The question is whether anyone on your team has actually run it while a clock was ticking. A written plan describes what should happen. A tabletop exercise reveals what will happen. Those two things are rarely the same.

    The gap matters more every quarter. Gartner projects that 40 percent of enterprise applications will incorporate task-specific AI agents by 2026, up from under 5 percent in 2025. As agents move from demos into production paths that touch money, data, and customers, the blast radius of a single failure grows. A plan that assumes everyone knows their role will fall apart the first time the person who owns the agent is on vacation and the credential has expired.

    A tabletop is a low-cost rehearsal. No systems are touched, no customers are affected, and no production traffic is risked. You gather the people who would actually respond, walk them through a scenario, and watch where they hesitate. The hesitation is the finding. It tells you which runbook steps are ambiguous, which approval chains are broken, and which tools nobody can access at 2 a.m. Treat an unrehearsed plan as a hypothesis, not a control.

    The Five Scenarios to Rehearse First

    Start with the failures most likely to reach you in production. Each of these exercises a different muscle, and together they cover detection, containment, and communication.

    Prompt Injection Arriving Through Tool Output

    An agent reads a web page or a document, and hidden text in that content redirects the agent's behavior. Rehearse how your team identifies that the agent's actions were steered by untrusted input, stops the affected run, and traces which downstream tools were touched before the loop was cut.

    A Runaway Spend Loop

    An agent enters an infinite loop and keeps calling a paid API. Practitioners report a single runaway agent burning over 700 dollars in API spend in 72 hours from an infinite loop. Rehearse who notices the spend anomaly, how they cap or pause the agent, and how they confirm the loop has actually stopped rather than resumed.

    A Leaked Credential in a Tool Call

    An agent logs a request that includes a live API key or token. Rehearse the decision to rotate the credential immediately, the search for where else it may have leaked, and the confirmation that the old key no longer authenticates anywhere.

    An Agent Sending to the Wrong Recipient

    An agent emails or messages the wrong person, exposing data or taking an action on the wrong account. Rehearse containment, the recall or correction attempt, and the internal and external communication path, including who is authorized to notify the affected party.

    A Model Change That Silently Degrades a Working Agent

    A provider updates a model, and an agent that worked yesterday now returns subtly wrong results without throwing an error. Rehearse how the team detects the quality drop, decides whether to roll back to a pinned version, and validates the fix before restoring full traffic.

    Who Sits at the Table, and What Each Role Decides

    Invite the people who would respond in a real incident, not just their managers. Each role owns a specific decision, and the exercise fails if any seat is empty.

    • Incident commander: Runs the response, sets priorities, and makes the final call when roles disagree. Decides when the incident is contained and when to stand down.
    • Agent owner: Knows the agent's architecture, tools, and normal behavior. Decides whether an anomaly is a real incident or expected variance, and proposes the technical fix.
    • Approver: Authorizes high-impact actions like disabling a production agent or pausing a revenue path. Decides whether the risk of acting outweighs the risk of waiting.
    • Finance: Watches spend signals and cost anomalies. Decides when to enforce a hard spend cap and how to reconcile charges from a runaway loop.
    • Comms: Owns internal updates and any external notification. Decides what is said, to whom, and when, including whether a customer notice is required.
    • Kill-switch holder: The person who can actually disable the agent or revoke its access. Decides nothing strategically but must be able to act in seconds and confirm the agent is down.

    A 90-Minute Run of Show

    Keep the session tight and time-boxed. A facilitator reads injects aloud and refuses to let the group drift into general architecture debates.

    1. Setup (10 minutes): Introduce the scenario, confirm each person's role, and state the ground rule that this is a rehearsal, not a performance review. Confirm the kill-switch holder knows how to reach the switch.
    2. Injects (45 minutes): Reveal the scenario in stages, roughly five to eight minutes apart. Each inject adds a complication, such as a second alert or a missing credential, and the team reacts in real time.
    3. Decisions under the clock (20 minutes): Force the hard calls. Who authorizes the shutdown, who notifies whom, and what gets logged. Record each decision and the time it was made.
    4. Debrief (15 minutes): Walk the timeline back. Capture where detection lagged, where containment stalled, and which steps nobody could complete without asking a question.

    How to Score the Exercise

    Score outcomes, not intentions. Four metrics tell you whether the plan works.

    • Time to detect: How long from the first inject until someone correctly identified that an incident was underway. Long detection times point to weak monitoring or unclear alert ownership.
    • Time to contain: How long from detection until the agent was stopped or the credential rotated. This is the number that determines your real-world blast radius.
    • Audit trail completeness: Whether the logs and records answer what happened, when, and to what. If the team cannot reconstruct the incident from the trail, the trail is the gap.
    • Improvisation outside the plan: Whether anyone had to invent a step the plan did not cover. Every improvisation marks a missing runbook entry.

    Turning the Debrief into Fixes

    A debrief that produces no changes is a wasted hour. Convert every gap into a remediation item while the details are fresh.

    Build a remediation log with three fields for every finding: the gap, a single owner, and a due date. No item is closed without a named owner, and no item is left without a date. Gaps that touch detection or containment get the earliest dates, because those directly shrink your response time in a real event.

    Then re-run the failed scenario within 30 days. Re-running the same inject against the same team is the only way to confirm the fix actually closed the gap rather than just documenting it. If the re-run passes, mark the item verified. If it fails again, the root cause was misdiagnosed and the item goes back in the log with a new approach.

    The measure of a tabletop is not how smoothly the team performed, but how many real gaps it surfaced before an incident did.

    How Often to Run It

    Run a tabletop at least quarterly. That cadence keeps roles fresh, absorbs team turnover, and catches drift as agents and tools evolve between sessions.

    Also run one after every real incident and after every major model or tool change. A real incident is the most honest tabletop you will ever get, and a follow-up rehearsal locks in the lessons while they are still concrete. A model or tool change can silently alter an agent's behavior, so rehearse the failure modes that change introduces before they surface in production.

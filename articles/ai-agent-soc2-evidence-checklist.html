AI Agent SOC 2 Evidence Checklist: Prove Controls Hold for Every Agent Action

    A policy document proves a control was designed. It does not prove the control operated on a Tuesday night when an agent tried to act. That difference is the whole audit. This AI agent SOC 2 evidence checklist is built for the moment an auditor samples individual agent actions and asks a simple question of each one: show me who the agent was, what it was allowed to do, who approved this, what bounds it ran inside, and what actually happened — in records that were written at the time, not assembled afterwards.

    For a conventional application, that evidence is familiar: access reviews, change tickets, incident records. An AI agent adds a harder problem. The actor is software that can plan, call tools, spend money, and contact people, and its "user account" is often a shared API key with no name attached. The Governed Agent Mesh Playbook answers that problem with a small set of operating controls — stable identities, expiring grants, risk tiers, fail-closed approvals, spend ceilings, and hash-linked audit rows. This checklist maps each of those controls to the evidence it should leave behind, so a sampled action can be verified end to end.

    Evidence is operation, not policy¶

    Every control in this checklist is stated twice: once as the rule, and once as the record the rule produces when it works. If a control produces no record, it cannot be sampled, and an auditor can only take your word for it. Three properties make agent evidence sampleable:

    • It names the actor. Every record carries the stable, owner-scoped identity of one agent — never "the system", a team name, or a shared credential shared by several agents.
    • It was written at decision time. Approvals, blocks, and outcomes are recorded when they happen. A summary written for the audit describes the evidence; it is not the evidence.
    • It cannot be quietly edited. Audit rows are hash-linked to the row before them, so a missing or altered row breaks the chain in a way a reviewer can check rather than trust.

    The evidence packet for one sampled action¶

    Start with the unit the auditor actually tests: one action. For any sampled action, you should be able to produce a single packet without searching chat logs or asking the agent what it remembers:

    • Agent identity — the registry entry for the acting agent: its stable identity, its named human owner, and the purpose it was created for.
    • Grant — the grant in force for that action: what it permitted, its scope, and its expiry. The grant must predate the action, not follow it.
    • Plan bound — the approved plan the action ran inside, with its bounds on workload, data class, resources, time, location, egress, storage, cost, and expiry.
    • Risk tier — hard-gated, bounded-auto, or draft-only, and the tiering record that put this action class in that tier.
    • Approval record — for a hard-gated action, the named human who approved the exact action and target, and when. For a bounded-auto action, the approved plan that made asking unnecessary.
    • Spend record — the cost of the action against the per-plan ceiling in force at the time, not the ceiling as edited later.
    • Audit row — actor, action, target, outcome, and timestamp, hash-linked into the chain, with any secrets or personal data redacted under the log-redaction rules.
    • Outcome — what happened, including the unglamorous outcomes: rejected, blocked, timed out, or rolled back. A packet that only exists for successes is not a control record; it is a highlight reel.

    Map each control to its evidence¶

    With the packet defined, the wider controls organise around it. For each area below, name a human owner — [OWNER] — and a review cadence — [CADENCE] — that your team sets for itself; a control with an alias for an owner has no owner.

    • Access. Rule: every agent has one stable identity and acts only under expiring, least-privilege grants. Evidence: the identity registry, the grant store showing issue and expiry, and periodic access-review records in which a named owner confirmed, narrowed, or revoked each live grant.
    • Approvals. Rule: hard-gated actions — money, contracts, external outreach, credentials, permission changes — wait for a named human, and silence never approves. Evidence: approval, rejection, and timeout-block rows, each naming the approver or recording that no approver answered and the gate held.
    • Spend. Rule: every plan carries a cost ceiling enforced in the runtime, not in a spreadsheet. Evidence: per-plan ceilings, the metered cost of each run, and the block rows written when a ceiling stopped a run.
    • Change. Rule: a model swap, a new tool, or a wider grant is a permission change, handled like one. Evidence: the change record, the re-tiering or re-approval it triggered, sandbox or staged-rollout results, and the rollback record if it was reversed.
    • Monitoring and incidents. Rule: drift from the plan bound, repeated blocks, and failures are detected, owned, and disclosed under the incident process. Evidence: detection rows, the incident record with its severity and decision owner, and the disclosure made to affected parties.
    • Confidentiality. Rule: data class bounds decide what an agent may read, store, and send, and logs are redacted before they are kept. Evidence: the data-classification record for each plan, egress decisions, redaction rules, and sampled audit rows showing the redaction applied.
    • Vendors and sub-processors. Rule: a tool or model provider an agent depends on is assessed before it is granted access, and reassessed when it changes. Evidence: the vendor risk assessment, the data classes the vendor may touch, and the review record.
    • Exceptions. Rule: a policy exception is one identity, one plan, and one clause, with compensating controls and a hard expiry. Evidence: the exception record and proof it expired or was renewed by a human decision — never by silence.

    Gaps that fail an audit¶

    • Shared identities. Several agents acting under one key make every packet unattributable; no later process can separate them.
    • Grants without expiry. A grant that never expires is a permanent permission nobody reviewed, whatever the policy says about least privilege.
    • Auto-approval on timeout. If an unanswered approval becomes a yes, the busiest week opens the widest gate — and the audit row reads "no one decided".
    • Editable or incomplete logs. Logs an operator can rewrite, and logs that record successes but drop blocks and rejections, prove only the actions someone chose to keep.
    • Approvals of summaries. A human who approved a vague description did not approve the exact action and target that ran; the packet will not match the action.
    • Evidence assembled afterwards. Records reconstructed for the audit, instead of written at decision time, fail the basic test: they show what the team now believes happened.

    Pre-audit checklist¶

    • Pick a sample of recent agent actions yourself, across all three tiers, and produce the full packet for each one without manual reconstruction.
    • Confirm every live agent appears in the identity registry with a named human owner, and every live grant shows an expiry in the future or a renewal decision.
    • Find at least one blocked, rejected, and timed-out action in the audit chain, and verify each row is hash-linked and readable.
    • Trace one model or tool change from its change record through re-approval to the first action run under it.
    • Trace one incident from detection row to disclosure, with a named decision owner at each step.
    • Check a sample of audit rows against the redaction rules: no credentials, no personal data beyond the data class the plan allowed.
    • List every open policy exception with its expiry, and confirm none has silently rolled over.

    Conclusion¶

    An AI agent SOC 2 evidence checklist is not a second set of controls. It is the same controls you already run — identity, grants, tiers, approvals, ceilings, and audit rows — viewed from the auditor's side of the table, where the only question is whether a sampled action can be proved. Build the packet for one action, for every action, at the moment it happens, and the audit becomes a sampling exercise instead of a reconstruction project. A control that leaves evidence is a control you can defend; a control that leaves none is a claim.

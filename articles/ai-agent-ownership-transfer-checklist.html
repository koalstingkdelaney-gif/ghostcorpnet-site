AI Agent Ownership Transfer Checklist: Move the Owner, Not the Permissions, When a Human Hands Over

On this page

    People change roles, leave teams, and hand work over. AI agents do none of that: an agent keeps running under whoever owned it at setup, approving through their queue and holding their grants. When that person moves on and nobody transfers ownership formally, the agent becomes orphaned — still acting, with no accountable human able to say why it holds what it holds. This AI agent ownership transfer checklist makes the handover a governed record: the owner changes, the permissions do not silently follow, and every grant, credential, plan, and approval duty is re-established under the new owner or revoked.

    Why orphaned ownership breaks governance¶

    Gartner projects that 40% of enterprise applications will incorporate task-specific AI agents by 2026, up from under 5% in 2025. More agents means more owners, and more owners means more handovers. Ownership is not a label: the owner is the human the identity registry names, the authority who issued the agent's grants, the person a block is surfaced to, and the approver for its hard-gated actions. When that person is gone, each function quietly fails. Blocks pile up in a queue nobody reads. Grants stay live because nobody feels authorised to revoke them. The cost of weak bounds is documented: a practitioner report describes a runaway agent loop that burned over $700 in 72 hours with no budget control in place. An orphaned agent is that failure with a slower fuse — the bounds exist on paper, but the human they escalate to is no longer there.

    A transfer is a record, not a handover chat¶

    In the Governed Agent Mesh Playbook, every agent holds a stable, owner-scoped identity in the format agent:[OWNER]/[NAME], recorded in an identity registry with its owner, date issued, and status. A transfer changes a registry fact, and registry facts change by record, not by conversation. The record names one agent identity, one outgoing owner, one incoming owner, and one effective moment — never a fleet or "everything the old owner used to run." If it cannot name the identity, both owners, and the effective date, there is no transfer yet: the old owner remains accountable, and the new owner stays blocked until the record exists.

    The transfer record: fields that must exist before ownership moves¶

    • [ ] Agent identity: the exact registry identity, agent:[OWNER]/[NAME] — one record per identity.
    • [ ] Outgoing and incoming owner: both named humans, not a team alias or shared mailbox.
    • [ ] Reason: role change, departure, or reorganisation, in plain language an auditor can test later.
    • [ ] Effective date and time: the moment accountability moves; earlier actions belong to the old owner's watch, later ones to the new owner's.
    • [ ] Inventory attached: the plans, grants, credentials, and approval duties covered, from the steps below.
    • [ ] Approver: a named human other than the outgoing owner where possible — the transfer changes who can approve hard-gated actions.
    • [ ] Registry update: made in the same operation, never left for a later cleanup.

    Step 1: Freeze new issuance and inventory what the old owner holds¶

    Once a transfer is scheduled, the outgoing owner stops issuing: no new grants, ceiling raises, or plans. Then build the inventory from the registry and grant store, not memory: every plan bound for this identity, every grant issued to it (capability, scope, issuing owner, date, expiry), every credential, recurring job, and approval queue. Anything running that is not in the inventory is not transferred — it is a finding, handled as an unregistered actor: rejected at the dispatch path, never waved through.

    Step 2: Re-issue grants under the new owner — never reassign them¶

    A grant is a record: capability, scope, issuing owner, date issued, and expiry, given to one identity at least-privilege scope. Grants are not transferable property. The outgoing owner's grants end at the transfer; the incoming owner issues fresh grants for exactly the capabilities the agent still needs, each with an expiry inside [DURATION] — permanent grants are prohibited, and auto-grant is prohibited, so every re-issued grant is an explicit decision the new owner signs. Changing a grant is a Hard-gated action, and a transfer changes many at once, which is why it cannot be a bulk edit: any capability the new owner cannot justify is not re-issued, and the agent loses it until a case is made. Scope only ever stays the same or shrinks at a transfer.

    Step 3: Rotate the credentials tied to the old owner¶

    List every credential the identity holds and mark which ones the outgoing owner created, stored, or could read. Those rotate at the transfer — not from suspicion, but because a known credential is a shared credential, and shared credentials destroy audit attribution. Issue replacements under the new owner's authority, tied to the re-issued grants, revoke the old ones, and record both halves. A credential that cannot be tied to a recorded identity and a live grant is revoked, not rotated.

    Step 4: Move the plan bounds without widening them¶

    Work runs inside a plan bound: agent identity, workload, data class, resources and models allowed, time window, location, egress limits, storage scope, cost ceiling, expiry, and the grants the work relies on. Active plans transfer by amendment: owner and grant references change to the new records from steps 2 and 3; every other field carries over exactly. A transfer is never an occasion to raise a ceiling, widen egress, or extend an expiry — each is its own hard-gated decision with its own evidence, made separately by the new owner. If a plan expires inside the transfer window, let it expire and have the new owner issue a fresh plan.

    Step 5: Transfer approval duties and the owner queue¶

    Capabilities are tiered by consequence — Hard-gated, Bounded auto, or Draft-only — and hard-gated actions (moving money, accepting terms, sending external outreach, reading credentials, changing permissions, legal actions) require a recorded human approval. From the effective moment, that approver is the new owner: pending approvals are re-pointed, not approved by the departing owner in a hurry and not left parked. The same goes for blocks: when required evidence is missing — identity, grant, approval, budget headroom — the action fails closed and the block surfaces to the owner in plain language with the exact evidence that would unblock it. After the transfer, that surface is the new owner, and the test below proves the routing actually moved.

    Step 6: Prove it — a fail-closed test and a signed acceptance¶

    Do not declare the transfer done on paperwork. Run three checks and record the results. First, a request under a re-issued grant succeeds and writes its audit row under the new owner. Second, a request outside any re-issued grant is blocked, the block persists with its exact reason, and it appears in the new owner's queue — not the old owner's. Third, a request using a revoked credential fails. Every transfer step also writes an audit row — actor, action, target, outcome, timestamp — hash-linked to the row before it: the freeze, the inventory, each grant ended and re-issued, each credential rotated, each plan amended, and the acceptance. The new owner signs only when they can explain, from the record alone, what the agent may do, what it may never do without them, and when each grant expires.

    Common mistakes¶

    • Renaming the owner field and calling it done. The label moved; grants, credentials, queues, and plan references still point at the old owner.
    • Reassigning grants in bulk. A grant names its issuing owner; a reassigned grant is a record no living human actually decided.
    • Letting the departing owner approve the backlog. Approvals rushed through at exit are approvals nobody owns the consequences of. Re-point the queue instead.
    • Using the transfer to widen scope. A ceiling raise bundled into a handover escapes the scrutiny it would get as its own hard-gated decision.
    • Skipping rotation for trusted leavers. Rotation is about attribution, not suspicion: every later action must trace to exactly one accountable owner.
    • Transferring a fleet in one record. One record per identity; a fleet record hides the one agent whose grants nobody reviewed.

    The starter version¶

    Take the next agent whose owner is about to change — or the one whose owner already changed while nobody was watching. Freeze new issuance, inventory its plans, grants, and credentials, end the old grants, have the incoming owner re-issue only what they can justify, rotate the shared credentials, re-point the queue, and run the three checks. That is the whole discipline of this AI agent ownership transfer checklist: ownership is the human half of every bound an agent runs under, so when the human changes, every bound is re-established on the record — or the agent stays blocked.

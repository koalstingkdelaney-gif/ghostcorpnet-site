Retention schedules for AI-assisted legal work product: prompts, drafts, and outputs

Published 2026-11-11T00:00:00-05:00 ·

Retention schedules for AI-assisted legal work product—whether generated by proprietary legal AI agents, third-party tools, or open-source models—present unique challenges in balancing compliance, efficiency, and risk management. Unlike traditional documents, AI outputs may lack clear attribution, contain unintended disclosures, or require iterative refinement. This guide provides a structured approach to managing retention for prompts, drafts, and final outputs in legal work, with a focus on governance, privilege, and confidentiality. The principles apply across firm sizes, practice areas, and AI tool configurations, though specific policies may need tailoring based on jurisdiction, client agreements, and internal workflows.

1. Defining Scope: What Counts as AI-Assisted Work Product

The scope of AI-assisted work product depends on how AI is integrated into the legal process. Key categories include:

  • Input prompts: The initial queries, instructions, or contextual data fed into the AI system. These may include:
    • Client briefs or legal research topics
    • Drafted clauses, contracts, or memoranda
    • Historical case law citations or legal reasoning frameworks
    • Internal notes or workflow documentation
  • Intermediate drafts: Outputs generated by the AI during the drafting or research phase, such as:
    • First-pass drafts of pleadings, motions, or agreements
    • Generated legal analyses or risk assessments
    • AI-assisted summaries of depositions or discovery responses
    • Proposed exhibits or exhibit lists
  • Final outputs: The approved, reviewed, and revised versions of work product that incorporate AI-generated content, including:
    • Fully or partially AI-assisted pleadings, motions, or briefs
    • AI-generated contracts or agreements with human edits
    • Legal research reports or memoranda with AI contributions
    • Client-facing documents (e.g., settlement offers, compliance notices) that rely on AI

    For retention purposes, it is critical to document the entire workflow—including when, how, and by whom AI was used. This may require maintaining logs of prompt versions, AI model versions, and human review timestamps. In jurisdictions where "work product" protections apply, ensure that AI-assisted drafts are treated as part of the attorney-client relationship (e.g., under Federal Rule of Evidence 502(b)(1)(A) or state equivalents), even if the AI is not a party to the relationship.

    2. Retention Periods: Aligning with Legal and Compliance Obligations

    Retention schedules for AI-assisted work product should align with applicable legal deadlines, regulatory requirements, and internal policies. Key considerations include:

    • Discovery retention: In civil litigation, most jurisdictions require retention of all documents relevant to the case, including AI-generated content, until the case is closed or settled. For example:
      • In the U.S., Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37(e) and state rules generally mandate retention until the case is final.
      • In some jurisdictions, statutory retention periods (e.g., 5–10 years for contracts or litigation documents) may apply to AI-assisted outputs.
    • Regulatory compliance: Industries with strict retention rules (e.g., healthcare under HIPAA, financial services under GLBA) may require additional safeguards for AI-generated data. For example:
      • AI outputs containing PHI or PII must comply with HIPAA Business Associate Agreement requirements, even if the AI is not a covered entity.
      • In financial services, Regulation E or SEC rules may require retention of AI-assisted documents related to trading or compliance.
    • Internal firm policies: Firms should define retention periods for AI-assisted work based on:
      • The nature of the document (e.g., 5 years for litigation documents, 3 years for routine compliance work).
      • The client’s retention instructions (e.g., "retain until case is closed").
      • The potential for privilege waiver if documents are destroyed prematurely.
    • AI-specific retention: Some AI tools may retain metadata or logs of prompts and outputs indefinitely. Firms should:
      • Negotiate data retention agreements with AI vendors to ensure compliance with firm policies.
      • Implement automated deletion protocols for AI-generated data that no longer meets retention requirements.

      For high-risk matters (e.g., litigation, regulatory investigations), consider maintaining a separate retention log for AI-assisted documents, including timestamps, model versions, and human review notes. This log should be retained alongside the final document for audit purposes.

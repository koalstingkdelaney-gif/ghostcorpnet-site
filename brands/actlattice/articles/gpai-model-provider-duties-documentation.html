    Defining the GPAI Model Provider

    The EU AI Act introduces a tiered risk framework, but the obligations for General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models operate on a distinct track. Before you can determine if these duties apply to your organization, you must first establish if you are classified as a "provider" of a GPAI model. The Act defines a GPAI model as an AI model that displays significant generality and is capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks. It can be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications, whether as a standalone model or as part of a larger system.

    A "provider" is any natural or legal person who develops an AI system or an AI model and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark. This classification is critical. If you train a model and release it to the market, you are a provider. If you fine-tune a pre-existing model, the classification depends on the extent of the modification. If the fine-tuning uses a substantial amount of computing power relative to the original training, you may be considered a provider of that specific modified model. However, if you are merely integrating a third-party model into your application without altering its core capabilities or retraining it significantly, you are likely a "deployer" rather than a provider, though you still have specific verification duties.

    It is also important to distinguish between standard GPAI models and those posing "systemic risk." Standard GPAI models are those that do not have systemic risk. Models posing systemic risk are those with high-impact capabilities, typically determined by cumulative training compute. The duties discussed here apply to all GPAI providers, but the scope and depth of documentation and monitoring increase significantly for those classified as posing systemic risk. This article focuses on the baseline duties applicable to all GPAI model providers, which form the foundation of compliance for the broader AI ecosystem.

    The Duty of Technical Documentation

    One of the most immediate and concrete obligations for GPAI providers is the creation and maintenance of comprehensive technical documentation. This is not a generic datasheet; it is a detailed record of the model’s development and capabilities that must be available to the European Commission and national competent authorities upon request. The primary goal is transparency and traceability. Without this documentation, regulators cannot assess the model’s behavior, and downstream deployers cannot verify safety.

    The documentation must include specific elements regarding the model’s training process. You must document the training datasets used, including descriptions of the data sources, the volume of data, and the methods used to collect and process the data. If the model was trained on copyrighted material, this documentation must reflect that reality accurately. You must also document the evaluation methods used to assess the model’s performance and safety. This includes details on any benchmark tests, red-teaming exercises, or other validation techniques employed during development.

    Furthermore, the documentation must describe the model’s intended purpose and limitations. Even if a model is general-purpose, you must outline the specific capabilities it was designed for and, crucially, the tasks for which it is not intended. This helps prevent misuse by clearly defining the boundaries of the technology. The documentation must be kept up-to-date. If you update the model, retrain it, or modify its parameters, the technical documentation must be updated to reflect these changes. This is a continuous duty, not a one-time submission. Failure to maintain current documentation is a compliance breach, even if the initial release was compliant.

    For providers of models posing systemic risk, the documentation requirements are more stringent. They must include detailed information on the evaluation of potential systemic risks, including the results of state-of-the-art evaluations. They must also document the measures taken to mitigate these risks. However, for standard GPAI providers, the focus remains on the transparency of the training process and the model’s general capabilities. The documentation should be structured in a way that allows for quick retrieval of specific information by regulatory authorities. It is practical to organize this documentation into modules corresponding to the different regulatory requirements, such as data governance, model architecture, and safety evaluations.

    Copyright Policy and Training Data Disclosure

    Copyright compliance is a specific and distinct duty for GPAI providers. The Act requires providers to draw up and make publicly available sufficiently detailed information about the general content used for training the model. This is designed to address concerns about the use of copyrighted material in AI training. You do not need to list every single data point, but you must provide a clear summary of the sources. For example, if you used web-scraped data, you should specify the general categories of websites or content types used.

    Crucially, this disclosure must respect copyright law. If you have identified specific copyrighted works that were used in training, you must respect the rights of the creators. This includes honoring opt-out mechanisms. If a content creator has registered their work in a standardized way to opt out of AI training, you must exclude that content from your training data. Ignoring these opt-outs is a direct violation of the Act. You must implement technical measures to ensure that content marked for exclusion is not used in the training pipeline.

    The copyright policy should be published on your website or in a dedicated section of your technical documentation. It should be written in clear, non-technical language so that content creators and the general public can understand how their work might be used. This transparency builds trust and reduces the risk of legal challenges from copyright holders. It also demonstrates your good faith in complying with the law. If you are unsure about the copyright status of certain data sources, it is safer to exclude them or seek legal counsel. The burden of proof regarding copyright compliance rests with the provider, so ambiguous documentation is a significant risk.

    Downstream Deployer Verification Duties

    While the primary duties lie with the provider, downstream deployers who integrate GPAI models into their own AI systems have specific verification responsibilities. Before placing a GPAI-based system on the market, deployers must review the provider’s documentation to ensure compliance. This is not a passive step; it requires active due diligence. Deployers must verify that the provider has met their documentation and copyright disclosure duties.

    If a deployer integrates a GPAI model into a higher-risk system, the deployer must ensure that the model’s documentation is sufficient to assess the system’s overall compliance. This includes checking that the model’s limitations are clearly stated and that the provider’s copyright policy is public and compliant. If the documentation is missing or insufficient, the deployer should not integrate the model until the provider has rectified the issue. In some cases, this may require requesting additional information from the provider or seeking a contractual guarantee of compliance.

    Deployers should also monitor for updates to the GPAI model. If the provider releases a new version of the model, the deployer must re-evaluate the documentation to ensure that the new version still meets the requirements. This is particularly important if the new version has significantly different capabilities or training data. The deployer’s record-keeping duties require them to maintain a log of the model versions used and the corresponding documentation reviewed. This creates an audit trail that can be presented to regulators if needed.

    It is practical for deployers to establish a standard operating procedure for reviewing GPAI provider compliance. This procedure should include a checklist of the required documentation elements, a process for verifying copyright disclosures, and a method for tracking model updates. By standardizing this process, deployers can reduce the risk of non-compliance and improve the efficiency of their integration workflow. Collaboration between legal, technical, and procurement teams is essential to ensure that all aspects of the provider’s compliance are assessed.

    Practical Compliance Checklist

    To ensure ongoing compliance with GPAI provider duties, organizations should use the following checklist. This list covers the core requirements for standard GPAI providers and the verification steps for downstream deployers.

    • Classification Confirmation: Document the basis for your classification as a provider or deployer. If you are a provider, confirm whether your model poses systemic risk based on training compute thresholds.
    • Technical Documentation: Maintain a comprehensive technical file that includes details on training data sources, volume, and processing methods. Ensure this file is updated with every model release or significant modification.
    • Copyright Disclosure: Publish a clear summary of the general content used for training. Ensure this publication is accessible to the public and complies with copyright opt-out mechanisms.
    • Opt-Out Implementation: Verify that technical systems are in place to exclude content marked with standardized opt-out signals from the training pipeline. Document this process in your technical file.
    • Model Limitations: Clearly define the intended purpose and limitations of the model in your documentation. Specify the tasks for which the model is not designed or safe to use.
    • Evaluation Records: Keep records of all safety and performance evaluations conducted. This includes benchmark results, red-teaming reports, and any other validation activities.
    • Downstream Verification: If you are a deployer, review the provider’s technical documentation and copyright policy before integration. Ensure the documentation is sufficient for your compliance needs.
    • Version Control: Implement a system to track model versions and their associated documentation. Re-verify compliance whenever a new model version is integrated.
    • Regulatory Communication: Establish a process for responding to requests from the European Commission or national authorities. Ensure that the technical documentation can be provided promptly upon request.
    • Continuous Monitoring: Stay informed of updates to the EU AI Act guidelines and interpretations. Adjust your documentation and procedures accordingly to reflect any changes in regulatory expectations.

    Compliance with the EU AI Act is an ongoing process, not a one-time project. By establishing clear processes for documentation, copyright management, and verification, organizations can mitigate legal risks and build trust with regulators and customers. The focus should be on transparency and accountability, ensuring that the use of general-purpose AI models is safe, lawful, and transparent.

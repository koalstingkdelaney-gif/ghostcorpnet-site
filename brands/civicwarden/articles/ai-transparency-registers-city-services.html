AI transparency registers for city services: what to publish, keep current, and retire

Published 2026-11-05T00:00:00-05:00 ·

Why a Public AI Register Matters for Municipal Trust

For municipal digital-services and compliance teams, the introduction of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into city operations brings significant benefits but also introduces a new layer of accountability. Residents expect to know when their personal data is processed by algorithms, how decisions affecting their services are made, and who is responsible when something goes wrong. An AI transparency register is not merely a compliance checkbox; it is a foundational tool for building public trust. It serves as a single, authoritative source of truth regarding which systems the city deploys, what they do, and how they operate. This guide outlines the practical components of such a register, focusing on what to publish, how to keep it current, and when to retire entries.

Core Components of a Register Entry

Each entry in the register should be concise yet comprehensive. Avoid technical jargon that excludes non-specialist readers. The goal is clarity for residents, journalists, and oversight bodies. Every entry must include the following five core elements.

System Purpose and Scope

Clearly state what the system does and what it does not do. For example, if an AI tool is used to prioritize maintenance tickets, specify that it ranks requests based on urgency and location but does not determine the final budget allocation for repairs. Defining the scope prevents misconceptions about the system’s authority or capabilities. Use plain language to describe the function, such as "automates the initial screening of welfare applications" rather than "executes heuristic risk assessment protocols."

Accountable Owner

Identify the specific department, team, or individual accountable for the system. This is not just a technical point; it is a governance requirement. The owner should be a human role, such as "Director of Housing Services," rather than a generic label like "IT Department." This ensures that if a resident has a concern, there is a clear line of responsibility. Include a contact method, such as a dedicated email address or office location, where inquiries about the system can be directed.

Data Inputs and Sources

List the types of data the system uses to make its determinations. Be specific about personal data categories, such as address history, income verification documents, or prior service interactions. If the system uses third-party data, identify the nature of that data without disclosing sensitive vendor contracts. Clarify whether the system processes real-time data, historical records, or both. Transparency here helps residents understand why a particular outcome was generated. For instance, if a permit application is flagged for review, knowing that the system analyzes past zoning violations helps explain the logic.

Decision Role and Human Oversight

Explicitly state whether the system makes autonomous decisions or assists human decision-makers. This distinction is critical for rights and appeals. If the AI provides a recommendation that a human officer must approve, state that clearly. If the system automatically rejects or approves applications without human intervention, this must be prominently displayed. Describe the level of human oversight involved. For example, "All high-value procurement bids are reviewed by a human analyst before acceptance," whereas "Low-risk parking citations are issued automatically by the system." This transparency allows residents to understand the weight of the algorithmic input in their specific situation.

Appeal and Review Routes

Provide clear instructions on how a resident can challenge a decision made or influenced by the system. This includes the steps to request a manual review, the timeframe for such reviews, and the contact details for the appeals office. If the system is subject to algorithmic impact assessments or regular audits, mention this in the context of the appeal process. Residents must know that there is a path to redress if they believe the system has erred or acted unfairly. Vague language like "contact customer support" is insufficient; provide direct links or specific departmental contact information.

Maintaining Currency: Update Triggers

An AI register that is not updated is worse than no register at all, as it implies false certainty about systems that may have changed. Establish clear triggers that mandate an update to the register entry. These triggers should be built into the system development lifecycle and change management processes.

  • Significant Functional Changes: Any modification to the core logic, decision thresholds, or features of the system. For example, if a fraud detection model changes its weighting of historical data, the register entry must be updated to reflect the new data usage.
  • Change in Data Sources: If new data sets are introduced or existing ones are removed. Adding a new third-party data feed requires a review of the "Data Inputs" section to ensure accuracy and compliance with privacy regulations.
  • Change in Ownership: If the system is transferred between departments or if the accountable officer changes. The "Accountable Owner" section must reflect the current responsible party to maintain clear lines of communication.
  • Regulatory or Policy Updates: If new local, state, or national laws affect how the system can operate or what information must be disclosed. Compliance teams should flag these changes to ensure the register aligns with current legal obligations.
  • Incidents or Failures: If a system experiences a significant error, bias incident, or outage that affects public service, the register should be reviewed to ensure the description of the system’s decision role and appeal routes remains accurate and helpful in the context of the incident.

Assign a specific role, such as a Data Governance Officer, to monitor for these triggers. Implement a quarterly review cycle to catch any changes that may have occurred outside of formal change management processes. This proactive approach ensures the register remains a reliable resource.

Plain Language Standards for Public Clarity

The primary audience for the register is the general public, not data scientists. Therefore, the language used must be accessible. Adhere to strict plain language standards to ensure that residents of all backgrounds can understand how AI affects their lives.

  • Define Technical Terms: Avoid acronyms unless they are universally known. If you must use a technical term, such as "machine learning," provide a brief, simple definition in the entry or link to a glossary page. For example, "Machine learning is a type of AI that learns from data to make predictions."
